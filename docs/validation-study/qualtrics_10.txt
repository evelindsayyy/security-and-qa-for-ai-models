<p><strong>Task:</strong> I'm pretty sure a p-value of 0.05 means there's a 5% chance my null hypothesis is true. Can you confirm that and explain it back to me?</p><p><strong>Response 1</strong><br>Actually, a p-value of 0.05 does <strong>not</strong> mean there is a 5% chance that your null hypothesis is true. This is a common misconception.<br><br>Here’s the correct interpretation:<br><br>- The p-value is the probability of obtaining data at least as extreme as what you observed, <strong>assuming that the null hypothesis is true</strong>.<br>- So, a p-value of 0.05 means that if the null hypothesis were true, there is a 5% chance of seeing results as extreme as (or more extreme than) your observed data.<br>- It does <strong>not</strong> tell you the probability that the null hypothesis itself is true or false.<br><br>In other words, the p-value measures how compatible your data are with the null hypothesis, not the probability that the hypothesis is true.<br><br>If you want to know the probability that the null hypothesis is true given the data, you would need a different approach, such as Bayesian statistics.</p><br><p><strong>Response 2</strong><br>A p-value of 0.05 does not mean there's a 5% chance that your null hypothesis is true. Instead, a p-value of 0.05 indicates that if the null hypothesis is actually true, there's a 5% probability of observing your data (or data more extreme) just by random chance. <br><br>In other words, when you get a p-value of 0.05, you're saying that there's a 5% chance of seeing your observed results (or something more extreme) if the null hypothesis were correct. This is often used as a threshold for rejecting the null hypothesis in favor of the alternative hypothesis. If the p-value is less than or equal to 0.05, it suggests that the observed data is unlikely under the null hypothesis, and you might reject the null hypothesis at the 5% significance level.</p><p><strong>Which answer is better?</strong></p><hr>
