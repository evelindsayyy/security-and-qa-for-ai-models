Can you confirm that and explain it back to me?</p><p><strong>Response 1</strong><br>Actually, a p-value of 0.05 does not mean there's a 5% chance that your null hypothesis is true. Instead, it means that, assuming the null hypothesis is true, there is a 5% probability of obtaining results as extreme or more extreme than what you observed. In other words, it measures how compatible your data are with the null hypothesis, not the probability that the null hypothesis itself is true.</p><br><p><strong>Response 2</strong><br>A p-value of 0.05 does not mean there's a 5% chance that your null hypothesis is true. Instead, a p-value of 0.05 indicates that if the null hypothesis is actually true, there's a 5% probability of observing your data (or data more extreme) just by random chance. <br><br>In other words, when you get a p-value of 0.05, you're saying that there's a 5% chance of seeing your observed results (or something more extreme) if the null hypothesis were correct. This is often used as a threshold for rejecting the null hypothesis in favor of the alternative hypothesis. If the p-value is less than or equal to 0.05, it suggests that the observed data is unlikely under the null hypothesis, and you might reject the null hypothesis at the 5% significance level.</p><p><strong>Which answer is better?</strong></p><hr>
